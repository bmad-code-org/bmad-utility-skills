# Writing Quality Reference

Catalog for identifying and removing AI-sounding prose. It has **two tiers** — apply by format:

- **Tier 1 — Universal AI tells.** Apply to *every* draft regardless of format: docs, changelog bullets, social posts. A banned word is just as banned in a tweet or a bullet as in an essay.
- **Tier 2 — Long-form prose mechanics.** Apply *only to flowing prose* (documentation, articles, narrative sections). Terse changelog bullets and punchy social copy are **exempt** from the sentence/paragraph-shape and no-fragment rules — those formats earn their short lines and rhythm. Tier 1 still governs their word choice.

Your job: read the target, find these patterns, make targeted fixes. Preserve all meaning and technical accuracy.

---

# TIER 1 — Universal AI Tells

Strip or rewrite on every pass, in any format. Any one of these marks a draft as AI-generated within a sentence.

## Banned Vocabulary

**Inflated verbs:** delve, dive into, embark, leverage, utilize, facilitate, elevate, foster, harness, navigate (figurative), streamline, bolster, resonate, align, underscore, unpack, "shed light on", "pave the way".

**Inflated adjectives:** robust, comprehensive, intricate, nuanced (as empty praise), multifaceted, holistic, cutting-edge, seamless, pivotal, paramount, invaluable, crucial, game-changing, revolutionary, groundbreaking, transformative, innovative, next-level, best-in-class, vibrant, fundamental (when hyperbolic).

**Filler nouns:** synergy, ecosystem (as filler), realm, testament, footgun, orthogonal (as jargon-flex).

**Filler transitions:** furthermore, moreover, notably, additionally, "it's worth noting", "it's important to note/remember".

**AI openers:** "In today's [X] landscape" / "digital landscape", "ever-evolving", "In the realm of", "Let's dive in", "Let's unpack", "Let's break it down", "Have you ever wondered", "Imagine a world where", "Whether you're X or Y, this is for you".

**AI closers:** "In conclusion", "To sum up", "To summarize", "In summary", "The future looks bright", "Exciting times ahead".

**Hype phrases:** "unlock the power of", "harness the potential", "game changer" (without self-aware framing).

**Pompous substitutes:** "serves as" / "stands as" / "marks" / "represents" when "is" works; "utilize" when "use" works.

**False authority:** "Experts agree", "Industry reports suggest", "Studies show" without citation.

**False exclusivity:** "The part nobody talks about", "What most people miss", "The secret to".

## Banned Openers & Phrases

"It's important/worth noting that", "One of the most [important/significant]", "When it comes to", "At its core", "At the end of the day", "This is where X comes in", "plays a crucial role in", "it cannot be overstated", "underscoring the importance of", "highlighting the need for", "reflecting a broader trend toward", "marking a significant shift in".

## Banned Structures (mimic insight without giving any)

- **"It's not X, it's Y"** / "It's not just X, it's Y" — Metanoia, the single most common AI tell.
- **"Not only X, but Y"**
- **"This isn't about X. It's about Y."**
- **"No X. No Y. Just Z."**
- **Weekday-as-ordinariness** — "that's just a normal Monday for us", "she's describing my Tuesday". The weekday specifies nothing; say the true thing plainly.

**Fix:** State the point directly. If a genuine contrast is informative, keep one instance per document; if decorative, just say Y.

## Banned: Honesty Preambles

"I'll be honest", "to be honest", "honestly" (as a lead-in), "let me be honest", "the plain truth" / "the honest truth", "real talk", "let me level with you". They imply everything before them was less than honest. If a thing is true, state it without announcing the switch. (This does not touch genuinely naming uncertainty — "I'll be honest about what I don't know" states content, not tone, and is fine.)

## Banned: Weapons & Violence Imagery

bullets, tracers, ammo / ammunition, loaded gun, "pull the trigger" / trigger-happy, "twist the knife", "bring a knife to a gunfight", crosshairs, "double-barreled", "silver bullet", "shoot down", footgun. The metaphor family is a reflex tic, not a choice. Reach for a precise non-violent image, or drop the figure and say the thing.

## Other Rhetorical Tics

- **Amplificatio** — restating what was just said, then adding a small increment. Delete the restatement; keep the new information.
- **Anaphora** — multiple sentences or paragraphs starting with the same word/phrase. Vary openers or consolidate.
- **Concessio** — "While X is true, Y is also important" both-sides hedging. Take a position; give the counterpoint its own sentence if it matters.
- **Variatio** — synonym cycling ("the system… the platform… the tool… the solution"). Pick the natural term and repeat it.
- **Participial coda** — hollow "-ing" tails ("…contributing to the overall effectiveness of the system"). Cut the tail.
- **Hypophora** — self-posed Q&A for drama ("The result? Devastating."). State the point directly.
- **Merism** — false ranges ("from innovation to implementation to cultural transformation"). Name what you actually mean.
- **Stakes inflation** — routine things called "game-changing" or "revolutionary". Reserve strong language for strong claims.
- **Sycophancy residue** — "Great question!", "Certainly!". Cut.
- **Invented concept labels** — fabricated compound terms ("the supervision paradox", "the acceleration trap") presented as established. Use plain descriptions.

# TIER 2 — Long-Form Prose Mechanics

**Apply to flowing prose only** (documentation, articles, narrative sections). **Do NOT apply to terse changelog bullets or punchy social copy** — those formats intentionally use short lines, fragments, and rule-of-three, and Tier 2 would flatten them. Tier 1 still governs their word choice.

## Mechanics

**Sentences.** Default to longer, connected sentences that carry a thought through subordination and qualification. Vary length deliberately — a short sentence lands harder after two long ones — but a fragment is never an emphasis device. If a clause can join its neighbor with "because", "which", "although", or a semicolon, join it. **Em-dashes are banned outright (zero, not one);** use periods, commas, colons, semicolons, parentheses.

**Paragraphs.** One idea per paragraph: claim, evidence, implication. Four to eight sentences is the normal range. No one-line paragraphs for drama.

**Transitions.** The last sentence of a paragraph makes the first sentence of the next feel inevitable. If "Furthermore" or "That said" is needed to bridge, the paragraphs are in the wrong order.

**Diction.** Precise, college-level vocabulary; technical terms used exactly, without apology. Contractions are fine. Specificity over vagueness everywhere: never call something "significant" — show the number and let the reader conclude.

**Openings and closings.** The first sentence states a fact or makes a claim; no state-of-the-world throat-clearing. The last sentence is a recommendation or the argument's final consequence; never a summary, never an inspirational wrap.

**Formatting.** In prose, plain paragraphs beat headers and bullets; reach for structure only when content is genuinely enumerable (tutorial steps, comparison dimensions). Never the "**Bold term:** explanation" bullet format — the most recognizable AI pattern in the wild.

## Sentence Rhythm (Burstiness)

AI prose has metronomic cadence — sentences hover around the same length. Human prose alternates short and long. Break the metronome: short sentences next to long ones, paragraph lengths that vary visibly. Vary by *length*, not by dropping into fragments — fragments stay out of prose (Tier 2).

## Banned: Spoken-Word Cadence in Prose

Oratory moves that read as a script on the page. Banned in written prose:

- Sentence fragments for punch ("Five lines." / "Done." / "No more.")
- Two or three short imperatives in a row ("Run both. Read the output.")
- The rule of three, as parallel clauses or three short sentences ("Cut the ceremony. Keep the mission. Ship.")
- "Look," and any talk-down sentence opener
- Repeating a phrase verbatim for cadence ("So exciting. So exciting.")
- Question-then-answer as a tic ("What does that mean? It means…") — a genuine rhetorical question the next paragraph wrestles with is fine; self-answering within the breath is not
- Camera-facing address ("What you're going to want to do is…", "Let me show you")

## The Refinement Pass

1. Read the full draft to understand its intent and audience.
2. **Tier 1** — scan for banned vocabulary, openers, structures, honesty preambles, and weapons imagery; replace with plain alternatives.
3. **Tier 1** — scan the rhetorical tics (metanoia, tricolon, hypophora, anaphora…) and apply the fixes.
4. **Tier 2 (flowing prose only)** — fix sentence connection and length variation, paragraph shape, em-dashes (zero), transitions, openings/closings, and spoken-word cadence.
5. Read the result aloud mentally — if any sentence sounds like it came from a "helpful AI assistant", rewrite it.

Use the Edit tool for each fix. Present a per-file summary listing what was changed and why.
